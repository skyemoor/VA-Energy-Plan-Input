# § 15.2-2288.8. Special exceptions for solar photovoltaic projects

**Title 15.2 (Counties, Cities and Towns) › Chapter 22 (Planning, Subdivision and Zoning) ›
Article 7. Zoning**

Retrieved 2026-09-11. Amended 2026, cc. 1005 (SB347), 1068 (HB711).

Applies to any **ground-mounted solar facility of 1 MW or more** serving property other than where
it is located. Far broader than § 10.1-1197.5's Article 5 (150 MW permit-by-rule) scope.

---

## The two provisions that matter for agrivoltaics

### (A)(3) — agrivoltaics as grounds for a height VARIANCE

> "The height of solar panels shall not exceed 25 feet above ground when the arrays are at full
> tilt, **except in cases where a height variance is necessary to allow for agrivoltaics activity
> below or in proximity to the panels**. For purposes of this section, 'agrivoltaics' means the
> same as that term is defined in § 10.1-1197.5."

**The definition is used here as an ENABLING provision, not a restriction.** Agrivoltaics is
grounds to exceed an otherwise-binding height cap. It does not impose a design standard on projects
that do not need the variance — a standard-height array does not invoke this clause at all.

This also confirms the § 10.1-1197.5 definition is **not article-limited in practice**: it is
imported by reference into Title 15.2 zoning, which governs a much wider class of projects.

### (A)(9) — grazing and co-located crop production are contemplated alongside pollinator plantings

> "An ordinance may require up to 75 percent vegetative cover... **For projects or portions of
> projects not used for animal grazing, co-located crop production**, native and naturalized
> pollinator plant species, or native and naturalized meadow species shall be planted, except for
> in the area directly beneath panels, and maintained throughout the solar project's life."

Two things follow:

1. **Vegetative cover is mandatory anyway** — up to 75%, maintained for the project's life. Solar
   sites must be vegetated regardless, which supports the finding that grazing or forage adds no
   management burden that was not already required.
2. **Grazing and co-located crop production are treated as ordinary alternatives** to pollinator or
   meadow plantings, in the same list, with no additional design condition attached. The statute
   contemplates sheep or crops under an otherwise conventional array as a normal way to meet the
   vegetation requirement.

## Note on the earlier "prioritize energy yield" concern

An earlier draft of this analysis argued that standard-density single-axis tracking might fail
§ 10.1-1197.5 clause (i) — "designed to prioritize and sustain agricultural productivity" — because
such arrays are designed to prioritize energy.

**That reading is weaker than the alternative.** The relevant counterfactual is not an idealised
agrivoltaic design; it is conventional solar development, which removes the land from agriculture
entirely. Against that comparison, a configuration that keeps land in agricultural production
*does* prioritize and sustain agricultural productivity relative to what would otherwise happen to
it. Clause (v), "does not significantly displace farming activity," reads the same way.

§ 15.2-2288.8(A)(9) supports this: grazing and co-located crop production appear as ordinary
alternatives to pollinator plantings, with no implication that the array must be redesigned around
them.

Neither reading is authoritative — this is a legal question, the statute quantifies neither
"prioritize" nor "significantly displace," and the definition dates only from 2026 so there is no
interpretive practice yet. But the earlier framing overstated the risk and should not be repeated
as though settled.

## Scale note

At 4–6 acres/MW a project at the § 10.1-1197.5 150 MW ceiling occupies **600–900 acres**. Virginia's
average farm is **187 acres** (VDACS, 2022 Census of Agriculture).

So a single project at that cap spans roughly **3 to 5 average Virginia farms** and is typically a
multi-landowner aggregation. That bears on clause (iii), "part of a farm business that is
consistent with commercial agricultural production," and clause (vi), "flexibility for farmers to
adapt to market conditions" — both of which read naturally for one farm and less obviously for an
aggregation of four.

Not resolved here. The distribution of Virginia farm sizes around the 187-acre mean is not held;
the Census reports 1,367 farms of 1,000+ acres, so large single-owner sites exist but are a small
share of the 38,995 total.
