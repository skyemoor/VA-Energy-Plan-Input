# NSPM Case: Rural Economic Development

**Working note, 2026-09-11.** Basis for the non-energy impacts of the agrivoltaic siting
assumption, structured against the National Standard Practice Manual.

---

## 1. Why this belongs in the test at all

The NSPM's central move is that a cost-effectiveness test should reflect **the jurisdiction's own
applicable policy goals**, not a generic utility-cost framing. Impacts are included because policy
says they matter, not because they are easy to monetize.

Virginia's policy goals reach rural economic development explicitly. § 45.2-1710(B) requires the
Virginia Energy Plan to address economic development, and the § 58.1-2636 revenue-share mechanism
exists precisely because the General Assembly judged that rural localities hosting energy
infrastructure should capture value from it.

So these impacts are **in scope by Virginia's own stated policy**, and the burden is to quantify
them defensibly rather than to justify counting them.

**NSPM symmetry (Principle 3) cuts both ways here and must be honoured.** Rural benefits are
counted only if rural costs are too: land conversion, viewshed, decommissioning obligations, and
the local service burden during construction. This note covers the benefit side; the cost side is
owed and is listed in §7.

---

## 2. The channels, and which are defensible

Rural economic development from utility-scale solar runs through more channels than lease income,
and they differ sharply in how well they can be quantified. Presenting them as one undifferentiated
benefit would be the easiest thing to attack.

| # | channel | recipient | quantified? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Land lease income | landowner | **yes — sourced rates** |
| 2 | Income variance reduction | landowner | **yes — qualitative, robust** |
| 3 | Local revenue share (§ 58.1-2636) | county | **yes — statutory rate** |
| 4 | Retained agricultural production | landowner | no — evidence contested |
| 5 | Construction employment | local labour | no — not yet estimated |
| 6 | O&M employment | local labour | no — not yet estimated |
| 7 | Grazing co-location (reduced mowing) | operator/grazier | no — not yet estimated |
| 8 | Farmland retained against development conversion | region | no — counterfactual required |
| 9 | Pollinator habitat spillover | adjacent growers | no — highly site-specific |

**Only 1, 2 and 3 are carried as quantified.** The others are named so the estimate is understood
as conservative, not so they can be implied into the total.

---

## 3. Channel 1 — Land lease income

Virginia lease rates run **$1,200–2,500/acre/year**, materially above the **$500–700** national
average. At the 2045 Build to Zero solar build of ~173,781 MW and 4.1–6.2 firmed acres/MW:

| | figure |
|---|---:|
| firmed footprint | 712,501 – 1,084,392 acres |
| annual lease income | **$0.86B – $2.71B/yr** |

**This applies to all utility-scale acreage, not only the agrivoltaic share.** A landowner is paid
for hosting the array whether or not the siting permits continued farming. Restricting it to the
85% would understate the effect.

---

## 4. Channel 2 — Income variance reduction, and why it is the strongest claim

The obvious argument — that agrivoltaics lets a farmer earn lease income *and* keep farming — rests
on crop yield under panels. That evidence is genuinely contested, varies by crop, and **no
Virginia-specific field trial was identified**; the base is Delaware's Eastern Shore and other
out-of-state work.

The stronger claim does not depend on yield at all:

> A contracted per-acre lease payment is a hedge against drought, hail and commodity price risk
> that dryland row-crop farming does not have. The revenue is **contractually independent of that
> year's harvest outcome**.

This holds even for crops where the yield evidence is weak or negative. For an NSPM benefit case
it is the better claim because it concerns **income variance rather than income level**, and
variance is what drives farm failure. A farm does not usually fail on average income; it fails on a
bad year it cannot absorb.

**This should lead the case.** It is robust to the weakest part of the evidence base.

## 5. Channel 3 — Local revenue share, and the politics it was written for

§ 58.1-2636 permits a locality to assess up to **$1,400/MW AC on solar generation and a separate
$1,400/MW AC on storage capacity**, escalating 10% on 1 July 2026 and every five years after — four
escalations by 2045, giving **$2,049.74/MW**.

At the 2045 build:

| | MW | annual |
|---|---:|---:|
| solar | 173,781 | $356.2M |
| sodium-ion | 50,547 | $103.6M |
| iron-air | 36,359 | $74.5M |
| **total** | | **$534.3M/yr** |

**Storage is a third of this and is easy to miss** — the statute assesses it separately, and an
analysis counting only generation would understate the county benefit by that much.

### Why this channel matters politically

This is the mechanism aimed at the constituency that has been **rejecting** solar projects. The
2020 legislation exists because rural localities, per contemporaneous legal analysis, *"felt they
were being forced to subsidize the economic development of the more affluent parts of the state"* —
low rural property values plus a machinery-and-tools exemption meant hosting a facility worth tens
of millions produced little local revenue. *"The response of many rural localities was to reject a
number of worthwhile projects."*

Unlike lease income, which accrues to whoever owns the land, revenue share accrues to the
**county** — funding schools, roads and services for residents who host the infrastructure without
owning any of it. That distinction is what makes it a community benefit rather than a landowner
benefit.

### An asymmetry worth stating plainly

Projects of **5 MW or less and net-metered projects are exempt**. So distributed rooftop and canopy
generate **little or no** local revenue share. This is a real structural difference between the
utility-scale and distributed paths, and it cuts in favour of the 85% utility/PPA framing on rural
grounds specifically — while cutting against it on the transmission-deferral grounds that motivate
the distributed scenario. Both should be stated.

## 6. The land-use figure that will be challenged

The agrivoltaic portion of the 2045 build is **605,626 – 921,733 acres**, which is **7.9 – 12.0% of
Virginia's farmland** (~7.7M acres — *figure to be verified against USDA Census of Agriculture
before publication*).

Roughly one acre in ten. This must be stated before an opponent states it.

**It is also the strongest argument for the agrivoltaic assumption itself.** If a tenth of
Virginia's farmland were converted to conventional solar, that is a genuine agricultural land loss
and the opposition writes itself. If it is agrivoltaic, the land stays in production and gains a
contracted income stream. The 85% assumption is the difference between *"solar takes a tenth of
Virginia's farmland"* and *"a tenth of Virginia's farmland gains a second income stream."*

The spread between 695,000 and 1,043,000 acres is entirely the 4–6 acres/MW assumption — the
difference between 9% and 14% of farmland. That assumption is load-bearing and should be tightened
or presented at its low end as a design target.

## 7. What is owed before this is publishable

1. **The cost side (NSPM Principle 3).** Land conversion, viewshed, decommissioning, construction-
   period service burden. Counting rural benefits without rural costs fails symmetry and invites
   the obvious rebuttal.
2. **Verify Virginia farmland acreage** against the USDA Census of Agriculture. The ~7.7M figure is
   recalled, not sourced, and a percentage-of-farmland claim cannot rest on it.
3. **AC versus DC.** § 58.1-2636 measures nameplate in AC. If the modeled build is DC, revenue
   share figures are correspondingly high.
4. **Revenue share adoption rate.** The statute is a ceiling adopted by ordinance. The Weldon
   Cooper Center maintains an inventory of adopted ordinances; applying the maximum statewide is an
   upper bound and the actual adoption pattern should replace it.
5. **The 85% share is an assumption, not a result**, and every figure here inherits that. NREL
   counts 13 existing agrivoltaic projects in Virginia, so current practice is far below.
6. **Employment channels (5 and 6)** are the largest unquantified gap and the most commonly cited
   in rural economic development arguments. Worth estimating if time permits.
