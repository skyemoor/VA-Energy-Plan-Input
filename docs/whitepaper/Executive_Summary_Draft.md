## The question

Virginia's clean energy law was written in 2020 against a demand forecast that has since roughly
doubled, driven almost entirely by data centres. The targets did not change.

**This analysis asks whether they are still reachable, what each level of compliance costs, and
what happens if Virginia builds only what the law's own minimums require.**

## The statutory minimum does not hold its ground

Building only what the Code names — **16,100 megawatts of solar and 20,000 megawatts of storage** —
and meeting the rest with natural gas:

| | 2026 | 2030 | 2035 | 2040 | 2045 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Clean share of energy** | 47.4% | **48.6%** | 45.7% | 35.0% | **31.6%** |
| **Cost, $ billion per year** | 2.4 | 3.8 | 6.1 | 9.0 | **10.9** |
| Peak gas generation, MW | 12,224 | 12,224 | 13,307 | 16,556 | 16,751 |

**Compliance peaks around 2030 and then falls by a third, while annual cost rises more than
fourfold.**

The turning point is where a fixed target stops keeping pace with a growing system. The 16,100
megawatts arrive as the law intends; demand simply grows past them.

**Storage moves energy. It does not create it.** A fixed generation target against rising demand can
only lose ground, however much storage is paired with it — and this scenario pairs the full 20,000
megawatts the Code requires.

## Three findings the General Assembly should be aware of

### 1. The two solar requirements in the Code work against each other

**§ 56-585.5(D)(2)** sets a one-time capacity target: 16,100 megawatts of solar or onshore wind by
the end of 2035.

**§ 56-585.5(C)(2)**, as raised by the Distributed Generation Expansion Act in 2026, sets a
continuing *energy* obligation: 5% of the renewable portfolio requirement must come from resources
of one megawatt or less. That share grows every year, because the portfolio requirement itself rises
to 100% by 2045 and the energy it applies to keeps growing.

**By 2045 the small-resource obligation accounts for roughly 60% of new solar construction.** And
because rooftop and small-scale systems are fixed in place rather than tracking the sun, the same
nameplate capacity delivers about 4.4 terawatt-hours less energy each year.

**Neither provision is unreasonable on its own.** Together, under a fixed capacity target, they
reduce the energy the target delivers.

### 2. The mandated storage is insufficient, not idle

An earlier reading of this model suggested the 20,000 megawatts of storage the Code requires would
sit largely unused — capital spent for little return.

**That was wrong, and the correction matters.** Measured against Virginia's actual gas fleet rather
than an unlimited one, the storage discharges **13 terawatt-hours a year** and is working close to
continuously in the hours it can. **Eighteen terawatt-hours of demand still go unserved.**

The storage mandate is not oversized. It is undersized relative to what a fixed solar target leaves
it to cover.

### 3. Most of the new gas this scenario builds is an artefact of how the model is bounded

Virginia sits inside the PJM regional market and imports roughly a fifth of its electricity. **This
model does not allow imports** — every megawatt-hour must be generated in state.

**That single assumption accounts for most of the new gas capacity the scenario builds.** With no
imports, closing the gap takes about **6,500 megawatts** of new combined-cycle plant. Allowing
imports at Virginia's actual share, it takes about **2,000**.

**Neither figure is the answer.** Imports are dispatchable and shaped — available when the region has
surplus — so a simple demand reduction is more generous than reality. But in a genuinely tight year,
the region may be short exactly when Virginia is, and the imports may not arrive when the capacity
is most needed.

**The honest statement is the range**, and it is reported that way throughout.

## What the cost depends on most

Scenario 2 burns more than 130 terawatt-hours of natural gas a year by 2045, so **fuel price is its
single largest uncertainty.** Three independently published forecasts:

| | source | cost in 2045 |
|---|---|---:|
| Low | U.S. Energy Information Administration | **$8.6 billion/year** |
| Medium | Deloitte | **$10.9 billion/year** |
| High | Hughes / Post Carbon Institute | **$12.8 billion/year** |

**A 25% spread — and the clean share does not move at all.** Gas price changes what this pathway
costs, not what it achieves, because the build is fixed by statute rather than chosen for economy.

## What this analysis does and does not settle

**It does settle** which compliance levels are reachable, what each costs per year, and how sensitive
those costs are to fuel price and to how the system is bounded.

**It does not settle whether any particular plant should be built.** This is screening-level
analysis — the stage that narrows options before detailed study, not the stage that approves
construction. A procurement decision would require production-cost modelling with unit commitment,
forced outages and transmission, which is a different tool used later in the process.

**Every figure here is measured**, from Dominion's own hourly demand projections, published fuel
price forecasts, and the generating fleet as it actually exists and is scheduled to retire. Where an
assumption is doing significant work, the analysis says so and shows what the answer would be if the
assumption changed.
