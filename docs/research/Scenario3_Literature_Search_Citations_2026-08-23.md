## §10.2 — Germany: Marktprämie as a real precedent for this project's own Section K

9. Sandra Enkhardt. "Germany introduces new rules for solar remuneration during negative prices."

    Society*, April 16, 2015. https://energsustainsoc.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s13705-015-0040-1

    (2014 EEG revision); the Easter Monday 2019 (-€52.75/MWh) example.

13. FfE (Forschungsstelle für Energiewirtschaft). "Negative electricity prices — How many renewable

    https://www.ffe.de/en/publications/negative-electricity-prices-how-many-renewable-energy-systems-will-run-through/
    — Source for the "70.2 GW / 75.4% of PV systems have little or no incentive to react to market
    signals" finding — direct evidence for the scale of the "produce and forget" problem this project's

14. Modo Energy. "Solar Cannibalisation: Why Germany's summer glut is costing consumers." August 19,

    findings — real-world confirmation of the cannibalization risk already flagged in §8.8.

15. Gugler, Klaus; Haxhimusa, Adhurim; Liebensteiner, Mario. "Market premia for renewables in Germany:
    The effect on electricity prices." *Energy Economics*, March 4, 2022 (peer-reviewed).
    https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0140988322000573
    — The peer-reviewed, counterfactual-analysis source for the ~70% reduction in negative-price hours
    attributed to the MPS's introduction; the "produce and forget" terminology itself.

16. *(Chinese/international comparative study, journal name not independently confirmed beyond the

    https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2096511726000071
    — Cross-country comparison (Germany/Netherlands/Australia) explaining the Continuous Intraday

17. RatedPower. "Solarspitzengesetz: How it affects solar and negative pricing." November 4, 2025.
    https://ratedpower.com/blog/how-solarspitzengesetz-affects-solar/
    — Confirms the 15-minute-interval suspension rule and the 2 kW exemption threshold; states the

18. *(arXiv preprint, not peer-reviewed — flagged as such given the strong claims it makes)*.

## A note on source quality, disclosed rather than smoothed over

Not every source above carries equal weight, and this list is deliberately not presented as uniformly
authoritative:

- **Peer-reviewed/primary-regulatory sources** (the strongest tier): #11, #15 (peer-reviewed journal
  articles); #19-20 (a law-review article and an LBNL report); #22-25, #57 (official PSC/SCC/state
  agency documents); #43-45 (PNAS and NREL); #51-55, #58 (SEC filings and an official Dominion tool).
- **Industry-press/trade-publication sources** (real, generally reliable, but not peer-reviewed):
  most of §10.1, §10.4, §10.6-10.8, and #42, #46-49.
- **Explicitly flagged as lower-confidence**: #18 (a single, non-peer-reviewed arXiv preprint making
  strong claims about German emissions trends — not corroborated elsewhere in this search); #56 (a
  third-party solar-industry site, not an official Dominion/SCC source, for the specific 15%
  screening-threshold figure); #44 (full text inaccessible due to a robots.txt block — cited via its
  own abstract and OSTI record, not independently read in full).
